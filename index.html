How the model works

Each day, vectors move through the vineyard as a random walk with a tunable bias toward movement along rows. Vector numbers follow a summer-peaked seasonal curve, with an overwintering floor and immigration from field edges. A vector that feeds on an infected vine can acquire GRBV, becomes infectious after a latent period, and can then transmit to healthy vines. Infected vines stay infected for life because grapevines are perennial, so infections accumulate across seasons. Symptoms appear only after the symptom-onset date and a vine latent period. Roguing removes symptomatic vines on a chosen date, and replanted vines are healthy the following spring. Insecticide removes a fraction of vectors on a chosen date.

Literature anchors

GRBV was first observed in California around 2008 and was identified and described in the early 2010s (Al Rwahnih et al., 2013). Transmission studies showed that S. festinus transmits the virus persistently and circulatively, which supports the vector-driven spread modeled here (Sudarshana et al., 2015; Bahder et al., 2016). Symptoms on red cultivars include red blotching between leaf veins, downward leaf rolling, delayed ripening, and reduced sugar accumulation, most visible late in the season. Reported studies have not found GRBV to be seed-transmitted, so field spread is attributed mainly to vectors and to movement of infected planting material. Field surveys often describe clustered rather than random infection, which is consistent with the along-row movement bias here.

What is assumed

Vector densities, probabilities, latent periods, and management efficacies are not measured values for any specific vineyard. They are set to produce plausible dynamics and can be explored with the sliders. Citations are given from memory and should be checked against the primary literature before any research or management use. This is an exploration tool, not a forecast.
